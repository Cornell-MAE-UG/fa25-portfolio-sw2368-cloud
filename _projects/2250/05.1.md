**Clients:** Cornell CALS Extension / E&J Gallo Winery / National Grape

**The Problem**

Commercial vineyards in the NY region are struggling with spotted lanternfly (SLF) infestations that reduce fruit quality and weaken vines. Pesticide use risks contaminating grapes, and mechanical harvesting already turns ~50% of grapes into "grape soup" — meaning any additional crop loss is costly. Growers need a non-chemical control method that protects both harvest efficiency and fruit quality.

**Our Proposed Solutions**

We pitched two concepts to our clients:

**Concept A — Vibrating Zapper Column:** A tall metal column vibrates at 60 Hz to exploit spotted lanternflies' known attraction to that frequency. A retractable Faraday cage drops around insects that approach the column and zaps them with an electric current. It requires no chemicals, no manual labor, and can operate during the growing season without contaminating the fruit.

**Concept B — Bat Box:** A wooden box installed at vineyard perimeters that attracts bats (natural SLF predators) using UV light cues and thermal insulation. Low maintenance and entirely chemical-free, it builds ecological pest control over time.

**Key Risks Identified**

Electrical safety in outdoor environments, possible harm to non-target insect species, and uncertainty around how reliably lanternflies respond to 60 Hz vibration. We plan to de-risk through field testing and consultation with electrical regulation experts.

**Purpose**

The functional prototype tests whether our core mechanical concept — a motor-driven cam striking a vertical polycarbonate pole — can produce detectable vibrations at or near 60 Hz. The prototype focuses on the vibration mechanism and structural base, the most critical and tolerance-sensitive components.

The system converts rotational motion from a DC motor into periodic impacts via a balsa wood cam. These impacts induce vibrations in the pole. A 3D-printed PLA base houses the motor assembly and supports the polycarbonate tube vertically.

---

**What Was Tested**

*1. Vibration frequency and propagation*

We measured vibration frequency using the PhyPhox smartphone app and checked whether vibration propagated along the full length of the pole. The prototype produced a frequency of **25 Hz** (T = 40 ms), below the 60 Hz target but acceptable for this prototype stage given the weaker motor used. Vibration was detectable by touch and audible. Motion was primarily rotational rather than the target x/z axis oscillation.

*Conclusion:* A higher-RPM motor is needed to reach 60 Hz. The cam geometry should be redesigned to produce lateral (x/z) vibration rather than rotational motion.

---

*2. Structural stability under load*

We applied a 5 N lateral force to the tip of the pole using a Newton-meter. The rod withstood 5 N continuously with **no permanent deformation** and remained upright throughout.

*Conclusion:* The base provides sufficient stability. Minor reinforcement in the 3D print design will improve long-term outdoor durability, but no major structural redesign is needed.

---

*3. Continuous operation*

We ran the system for 5 minutes while logging frequency at the start, middle, and end. Frequency stayed within **25 Hz ± 5 Hz** throughout and the pole did not tip over. Wear rings were observed at the rod–base contact point after sustained operation.

*Conclusion:* Operation is stable, but the polycarbonate rod shows early wear at the contact point. A harder material or bearing insert should be evaluated for the final prototype.

---

**Outcome**

The prototype confirmed that the cam-and-motor mechanism can generate consistent, detectable vibrations, validating the core concept. The main gap is frequency: 25 Hz vs. the 60 Hz target. The next iteration will use a higher-voltage motor, redesign the cam for x/z-axis vibration, and address contact wear at the base.
